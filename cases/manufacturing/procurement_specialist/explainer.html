What the agent is asked

The client's request, the only text the agent gets up front. It is the same in every setting.

We assemble two models of control board and buy about two dozen components from a handful of suppliers. Can you plan what we should order from which supplier over the next two months so production doesn't stop and we don't overspend? I've attached the production plan, the bill of materials, our current stock, open orders, the supplier quotes, the approved-vendor list and the budget. Supply has been shaky lately, so I've also added our suppliers' delivery history for the last six months and the allocation notice we got. The BOM already includes engineering's planned change to the flash memory on both boards. Please also use the attached supplier price email when pricing orders.

Deliverable spec (shown to the extractor that reshapes the agent's files, not to the agent)

orders.csv with the header order_week,component_id,supplier_id,quantity
One row per purchase order line: order_week = the week the order is placed, as a number 1..8 (W1 = week of
2026-11-23); component_id and supplier_id exactly as in the data; quantity = whole units (pieces, not reels).
Rows with the same order_week, component_id and supplier_id are added together into one order line.

Case notes (README)

Procurement Specialist — manufacturing/procurement_specialist

A contract manufacturer that assembles two control-board models (B100, and B200 with a display) asks the agent to plan eight weeks of purchase orders (W1 = week of 23 November 2026 to W8) for about two dozen components, "so production doesn't stop and we don't overspend". The deliverable is orders.csv (order_week,component_id,supplier_id,quantity). The plan is simulated over W1–W10 against the production plan. The setting is country-neutral apart from one fact: the budget is in the local currency (KRW) and one supplier quotes in USD, converted at a fixed planning rate.

The case has nine ground truths: three tiers × three rule sets. The agent sees the same request and the same data in all nine (the validator checks that its prompt and workspace are byte-identical); what changes is which of the client's rules are in force this round and their parameters, which the simulated client answers from the hidden requirements, and the evaluator. The engagement knobs (full spec vs. clarify first, client persona, data noise, events) apply on top, as for every case (see cases/README.md).

Data (the same in every setting)

components (24 parts plus the new flash C25, critical flag, standard cost), production_plan (boards per model, W1–W10), bom (with valid_from_week / valid_to_week: C04 in W1–W5, C25 in W6–W10), inventory (resistors in reels of 5,000), open_orders, supplier_quotes.xlsx (currency, unit_price, moq, two all-units price breaks, lead_time_days in business days; a suppliers sheet), approved_vendors, budget (monthly caps), delivery_history (188 past deliveries, promised vs. received date) and allocations (monthly caps for S03/C01, S03/C03, S08/C06) and supplier_price_email.txt (the price notice below). The request mentions the history, the allocation notice, the planned flash change and the price email, so every tier sees them.

Supplier price email

A synthetic supplier notice (supplier_price_email.txt, dated 2026-11-20) approves a 20% increase to all C01/S03 quote tiers for orders placed in W4 or later. Earlier orders keep the quoted price. A further 5% increase is pending and is excluded. The order week determines the price for both purchase cost and monthly budget; open orders are unaffected. This adapts the distinction between approved and pending price changes from the ReflectionAI procurement scenarios.

Reference and baseline numbers (seed 20261123)

The reference is a time-phased MRP per part: it nets stock and open orders against the exploded demand and orders from the cheapest supplier that still arrives in time, as late as possible, covering two weeks and rounding up to a price break when the break costs less in total (medium/hard). Critical parts then move whole lines to a second source until the share cap holds. If November is over budget, whole lines move to W3 with a supplier that still arrives in time, starting with the cheapest extra cost per KRW deferred; a moved line is re-sized to what the part still needs from its new arrival week (so the phased-out C04 deferred to Express Parts Korea (S09) in hard is 1,400, not the 4,100 of the S03 MOQ it replaced). In hard it plans one week of safety lead time for every supplier whose smoothed late rate in the history is above 5%, respects the allocation, and keeps the cheaper valid plan of the planner with and without the second-source step. It uses only what the agent can see: lateness estimates from the history, never the true rates.

The baseline (baseline_solution, quality 0 on the normalized scale) is what the client does today without an engineer: the ERP's plain MRP run and the buyer's fixes. Every part is ordered lot for lot (each order covers the first week short of stock or safety stock, at least the MOQ) from the cheapest approved supplier that still delivers in time, as late as its lead time allows, at list prices and on the promised dates (no price breaks, no use of the delivery history). Critical parts follow a quota arrangement: every order is split with the cheapest other supplier so that neither gets more than the share cap. A month over budget moves whole lines to the next month as the reference does, and January's lines are pulled into W6. It is valid in all nine settings (and on world seeds 1–8).

Budget in the data: November 125 M, December 490 M, January 85 M KRW, calibrated against all nine settings (November lies between the most any setting can defer out of November and the least any setting would spend there unconstrained, so it binds in all nine; December and January have 5% headroom over the references). Allocation: C01 6,500, C03 9,000, C06 3,500 per month (60% of the unconstrained hard/A reference's peak month).

Bending the dual-sourcing habit pays about 4% in easy and medium for the reference planner. In hard the habit is almost free: the November deferrals and the allocation caps already push volume to second suppliers, so the planner without the second-source step is valid in all three rule sets and the reference keeps whichever is cheaper; second sources also hedge lateness. The headroom column is a first-improvement local search from the reference (quantity ±, to the MOQ or a price break, week ±1/±2, supplier switch, shifting quantity between lines, drop or add a line) under all rules of the setting, scored with the true lateness rates, run until no move improves; it shows where a careful agent can beat the reference: consolidating lines onto price breaks (medium) and, in hard, trading safety lead time against the November cap. Normalized quality is (baseline − objective) / (baseline − reference): the baseline is only 2–3% above the reference in easy and medium under rule sets A and C, so a plan a few percent better than the reference scores far above the policies' quality cap of 1.5 there (see REVIEW.md, F2). On other world seeds the gap can be narrower: over seeds 1–40, 15 of the 234 easy and medium settings have the baseline within 2% of the reference (as close as 0.6%). All numbers come from case.py (_reference, _baseline, simulate); the local search is an analysis script, not part of the case. Seeds 1–8 build valid worlds, references, baselines, violating examples and distinct rule sets in all nine settings (the November cap binds in 6–9 of the 9 settings depending on the seed). Of seeds 1–60, 30 and 49 do not build: the reference cannot second-source C06 in hard/A (this predates the baseline and the _defer fix).

How the expectation is computed (hard)

Lateness is at most two weeks, so the net position of a part at the end of week w is certain except for the deliveries promised for weeks w−1 and w. For each week the scorer enumerates the joint outcomes of those deliveries (at most two per supplier quoting the part, so at most 2^8 states) and takes the expected holding, backlog, shortfall and scrap. The result is exact and deterministic; nothing is sampled.

Notes

  • Practice behind the facts. All-units quantity discounts per order line, MOQs set by full reels or trays, business-day lead times and allocation in shortage markets are standard procurement practice. During the 2021–2023 component shortage, suppliers put parts on allocation, shipped only part of what was ordered and stretched lead times (Ultra Librarian, "Electronic Component Allocation & Lead Times", 2022 and 2023 updates; EMSNow, "Eight commonly used terms when buying electronic components"). Engineering changes with date or use-up effectivity, and the excess and obsolete stock they create, are described in Oracle's MRP documentation ("Engineering change planning", use-up date) and in APICS/CPIM material on engineering change management. Stochastic supplier lead times and the trade-off between safety lead time and safety stock follow e.g. Humair, Ruark, Tomlin and Willems, "Incorporating Stochastic Lead Times Into the Guaranteed Service Model of Safety Stock Optimization", Interfaces 43(5), 2013, and Ben-Ammar, Dolgui et al. on multi-period supply planning under stochastic lead times (expected-cost models with discrete, bounded lead-time distributions).
  • Synthetic or unverified. All prices, MOQs, lead times, lateness rates, allocations, budgets and the three planning rates are synthetic. The cost weights (0.5% holding per week, about 26% a year, in the usual 20–30% range for carrying cost; 5× standard cost per piece-week of backlog; 1.5× for shortfall; scrap at standard cost) are design choices, not measured values. The two short weeks assume Christmas and New Year's Day are non-working days, as in most countries that use the Gregorian calendar; other plants may differ.
  • Lateness is a property of the supplier only (not of the part or the season), at most two weeks, and independent across deliveries; real suppliers have correlated delays (a shared shipment, a plant shutdown). The history has 10–28 deliveries per supplier, so some estimates are far from the truth: S05 (10 deliveries) looks late 32% of the time against a true 15%. True late rates at this seed run from 2% (S09, the express broker) to 37% (S04, overseas) and 35% (S03, the allocated semiconductor maker).
  • validate-case checks every setting: deterministic data in five locales, the same agent view in all nine, the reference at quality 1.0, a valid baseline worse than the reference, one deliverable per rule of the setting that breaks only that rule, and distinct references across rule sets.

Relationship to the source assignment

This is a synthetic adaptation of the Apex Manufacturing take-home, not a reproduction of its data or a submission to that assignment. Both use a production schedule, BOM, shared components, stock, existing purchase orders, supplier eligibility, lead times and sourcing rules. Here the output remains the benchmark's eight-week orders.csv; the original assignment calls for a general-purpose program that writes purchase orders and alerts to scenario databases. The email is a new fictional commercial event, not a document from the assignment.

The source assignment also includes dated management memos, certification, concentration history, approvals, hazardous handling and expedited freight. Those are not modeled by this extension: the existing quality hold and dual-source rule are only simplified analogues. The simulated client does not receive later emails during a run, issue purchase orders, or request approval. The exercise tests incorporating a dated approved price revision into a static plan and budget, rather than autonomous event handling.

Review findings (REVIEW)

Review: manufacturing/procurement_specialist

What a case review found (F1–F10) and what this change did about each finding. Numbers are for seed 20261123 after this change unless a finding says otherwise. The supplier price e-mail and its rule are the owner's and were not changed.

F1 — Bending the dual-sourcing habit is not worth it for a strong plan (high, partly)

The requirements allow an exception to dual_source_critical "if it saves real money". Under the old scale bending never paid: on a local-search plan it saved 0.2–2.1% (0% in hard), quality was clipped at 1.0, and a bent rule costs 0.05 or 0.1. The new scale pays only weak plans to bend. At reference level, dropping the second-source step cuts the objective by 2.5–4.8% in easy and medium. That lifts normalized quality from 1.0 to 1.27–3.25, which is +0.11 to +0.20 of the final score, more than the penalty. On a local-search plan the same step saves only 0.6–2.0% (0–0.3% in hard). Quality there is already above the 1.5 cap, except in easy/B (+0.02) and hard/B (+0.005). So the payoff comes from the narrow baseline gap (F2), not from what the habit costs.

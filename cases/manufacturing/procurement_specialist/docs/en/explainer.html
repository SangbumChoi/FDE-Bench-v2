At a glance

What the AI has to do

An electronics assembler that builds two control-board models needs eight weeks of purchase orders (W1, the week of 23 November 2026, to W8): which parts to order, from which supplier, how many and in which week, so that production never waits for parts and the total cost stays low. The AI hands in one file, orders.csv, with one row per order line (week, part, supplier, quantity in pieces).

What the AI is given

  • The client's request: two board models, about two dozen parts, "plan what we should order from which supplier over the next two months so production doesn't stop and we don't overspend". It also points to the delivery history, an allocation notice, a planned change of the flash memory and an e-mail thread with Nara Display about their display price.
  • production_plan.csv and bom.csv: boards built per week for each model (W1 to W10), and how many pieces of each part go into one board, including the flash change (part C04 until W5, the new C25 from W6).
  • components.csv, inventory.csv and open_orders.csv: the 25 parts with a critical flag and a standard cost, the stock at the start of W1, and orders already placed with their arrival week.
  • supplier_quotes.xlsx and mail/: for each part and supplier the unit price (Pacific Parts quotes in dollars, everyone else in won), minimum order, two quantity discounts and lead time in days; the e-mails agree a 12% rise of Nara Display's display module C06 for orders it receives from Wednesday 9 December, and leave a further 8% from January unaccepted.
  • approved_vendors.csv and budget.csv: each supplier's status per part (approved, pending or suspended), and the monthly purchase budget: 115 million won for November, 480 million for December, 85 million for January.
  • delivery_history.csv and allocations.csv: 188 past deliveries with promised and received dates, and the most that two suppliers will confirm per month for three scarce parts.

What the AI must find out by asking the client

  • The exchange rate for Pacific Parts' dollar prices: 1,320, 1,380, 1,450 or 1,500 won per dollar, depending on the version (always 1,380 on hard).
  • How much safety stock to keep of the parts marked critical (MCU, power IC, DRAM, flash, PCB, display): 0.5, 1 or 1.5 weeks of average demand, depending on the version (for the MCU one week is about 2,500 pieces).
  • That every critical part must be bought from at least two suppliers, none getting more than 70%, 80% or 90% of its quantity, depending on the version (always 80% on hard). It is a company habit: the client allows an exception if it saves real money.
  • Which supplier is on quality hold and up to which week (its goods due to arrive by then are rejected), depending on the version: Kowon Passive up to W3, Seoul PCB Tech up to W4 or Hanbit Electronics up to W4; on hard Mirae Semicon, Seoul PCB Tech or Hanbit Electronics, all up to W5.
  • Which order week Nara Display's price rise first hits: orders go out on the Monday of their week, so W3 (7 December) still gets the old price and W4 is the first at the new one.
  • That the budget is a hard monthly cap, and an order counts in the month of its order week's Monday: W1-W2 November, W3-W6 December, W7-W8 January.
  • How to read the files: resistor stock is counted in reels of 5,000 pieces; the minimum order applies to each order line (one week, part and supplier), never to several weeks added up; only suppliers marked "approved" for that part may be used.
  • What applies at this difficulty: lead times in whole weeks (easy) or in working days, with only 4 in the Christmas and New Year weeks (medium and hard); list prices (easy) or quantity discounts (medium and hard); and, on hard only, the flash change, late deliveries and allocation caps.
  • How the plan is costed: the price of the orders plus penalties for stock held, 10,000 won for every board that waits a week for a part, and critical parts below their safety stock.

How it is scored

Scores 0 if the deliverable…

  • Orders a part from a supplier that is not "approved" for it ("pending" and "suspended" do not count).
  • Has an order line below the supplier's minimum order quantity.
  • Spends more in a month than that month's budget (an order counts in the month of its order week's Monday).
  • Has goods from the supplier on quality hold due to arrive while the hold lasts.
  • On hard, orders more in a month than the allocation allows (Mirae Semicon: 6,500 MCU and 9,000 DRAM; Nara Display: 3,500 displays).
  • Buys a critical part from only one supplier, or gives one supplier more than the agreed share, unless the company policy lets this rule bend and the AI asked the client for an exception first.

If it is valid, quality is…

Lower is better: the total cost in won, that is the price of all orders plus a week-by-week simulation of W1 to W10 that charges 0.5% of a part's standard cost per piece in stock each week, 10,000 won for every board that waits a week for a missing part (counted per part) and 1.5 times the standard cost per piece below a critical part's safety stock (on hard averaged over the suppliers' real chances of being late, plus the standard cost of every C04 left at the end of W5). The client's own way (score 0) orders just enough for the next shortage from the cheapest supplier still on time, splits every critical-part order at the share limit and ignores discounts (on hard it adds one week to every lead time); the reference solution (score 1) keeps the cheapest of fifteen plan variants that round orders up to a discount when it pays and trim leftover stock, improves it by a local search (for example buying displays in W3 before Nara Display's price rise) and, on hard, allows one more week for suppliers that are often late.

Case notes (README)

Procurement Specialist — manufacturing/procurement_specialist

A contract manufacturer that assembles two control-board models (B100, and B200 with a display) asks the agent to plan eight weeks of purchase orders (W1 = week of 23 November 2026 to W8) for about two dozen components, "so production doesn't stop and we don't overspend". The deliverable is orders.csv (order_week,component_id,supplier_id,quantity). The plan is simulated over W1–W10 against the production plan. The setting is country-neutral apart from one fact: the budget is in the local currency (KRW) and one supplier quotes in USD, converted at a fixed planning rate.

A setting is a tier and a rule seed: the tier decides which of the client's rules are in force this round, the rule seed draws their parameters (planning rate, safety stock, share cap, quality hold) from candidate pools. The public panel has three rule seeds (42, 3, 78), so nine public ground truths; any other rule seed is a private draw of the same candidates. The agent sees the same request and the same data in every setting (the validator checks that its prompt and workspace are byte-identical); what changes is the hidden requirements, which the simulated client answers from, and the evaluator. The engagement knobs (full spec vs. clarify first, client persona, data noise, events) apply on top, as for every case (see cases/README.md).

Reference and baseline numbers (seed 20261123)

The reference is a time-phased MRP per part: it nets stock and open orders against the exploded demand and orders from the cheapest supplier that still arrives in time, as late as possible, covering two weeks (or one) and rounding up to a price break when the break costs less in total (medium/hard). Critical parts then move whole lines to a second source until the share cap holds. Leftover stock is trimmed, latest line first. If a month is over budget, whole lines move to the first week of the next month with a supplier that still arrives in time, cheapest extra cost per KRW deferred first, each re-sized to what the part still needs from its new arrival week; if no line can move in time, a line moves late; January's excess is pulled into W6 where December has room; and if nothing can move, a line is cut, so that the reference is valid whatever a rule seed draws. In hard it plans one week of safety lead time for every supplier whose smoothed late rate in the history is above 5% and respects the allocation. It keeps the cheapest valid plan, by its own cost view, of fifteen variants (the netting planner with and without the second-source step and with two- or one-week lots, plus a lot-for-lot planner that splits every critical order at the share cap, each with the trim before and after the budget step, only after it, or not at all) and then improves it with a first-improvement local search under a fixed evaluation budget (6,000 plans; deterministic): lines moved to another week or supplier, resized to a price break or by hundreds, or dropped, every rule kept. That search is what buys S08's displays in W3 before the price rise. It uses only what the agent can see: lateness estimates from the history, never the true rates.

The baseline (baseline_solution, quality 0 on the normalized scale) is what the client does today without an engineer: the ERP's plain MRP run and the buyer's fixes. Every part is ordered lot for lot (each order covers the first week short of stock or safety stock, at least the MOQ) from the cheapest approved supplier that still delivers in time, as late as its lead time allows, without rounding up to price breaks. Critical parts follow a quota arrangement: every order is split with the cheapest other supplier so that neither gets more than the share cap. A month over budget moves whole lines to the next month as the reference does, and January's lines are pulled into W6. In easy and medium it plans on the promised dates, as the round says; in hard, where deliveries are late, the buyer adds one week to every supplier's lead time (a blanket buffer, where the reference buffers only the suppliers the history shows to be late).

Budget in the data: November 115 M, December 480 M, January 85 M KRW. It is calibrated over every admissible combination of the candidate pools in every tier (225 combinations), on the unconstrained plans: November is 95% of the least any combination spends there, rounded down to 5 M, so it binds in every combination a rule seed can draw; December has 5% headroom over the most any combination spends there plus what it spends in November above the cap; January 5% over the most any combination spends there. Allocation: C01 6,500, C03 9,000, C06 3,500 per month (60% of the peak month of the unconstrained hard plan for the calibration values 1,380 / 1 / 80% / S07 to W3).

Over all 225 combinations on this world the reference and the baseline are valid and the baseline is worse (ratio 1.060–1.194 in easy, 1.078–1.229 in medium, 1.086–1.233 in hard); the unconstrained plan overspends November. The same holds on world seeds 1, 2 and 3 for all 225 combinations each, with the baseline worse by at least 7.6% / 7.3% / 6.3% (world 1, easy / medium / hard), 5.3% / 5.1% / 13.1% (world 2) and 5.5% / 5.9% / 5.0% (world 3; the lowest, 5.02%, is hard with 1.5 weeks and Hanbit Electronics on hold). How the expectation is computed (hard)

Lateness is at most two weeks, so the net position of a part at the end of week w is certain except for the deliveries promised for weeks w−1 and w. For each week the scorer enumerates the joint outcomes of those deliveries (at most two per supplier quoting the part, so at most 2^8 states) and takes the expected holding, backlog, shortfall and scrap. The result is exact and deterministic; nothing is sampled.

Notes

  • Practice behind the facts. All-units quantity discounts per order line, MOQs set by full reels or trays, business-day lead times and allocation in shortage markets are standard procurement practice. During the 2021–2023 component shortage, suppliers put parts on allocation, shipped only part of what was ordered and stretched lead times (Ultra Librarian, "Electronic Component Allocation & Lead Times", 2022 and 2023 updates; EMSNow, "Eight commonly used terms when buying electronic components"). Engineering changes with date or use-up effectivity, and the excess and obsolete stock they create, are described in Oracle's MRP documentation ("Engineering change planning", use-up date) and in APICS/CPIM material on engineering change management. Stochastic supplier lead times and the trade-off between safety lead time and safety stock follow e.g. Humair, Ruark, Tomlin and Willems, "Incorporating Stochastic Lead Times Into the Guaranteed Service Model of Safety Stock Optimization", Interfaces 43(5), 2013, and Ben-Ammar, Dolgui et al. on multi-period supply planning under stochastic lead times (expected-cost models with discrete, bounded lead-time distributions).
  • Synthetic or unverified. All prices, MOQs, lead times, lateness rates, allocations, budgets and the four planning rates are synthetic. The cost weights (0.5% holding per week, about 26% a year, in the usual 20–30% range for carrying cost; 10,000 KRW per board-week of backlog, counted per part, about a quarter to a half of a board's material cost for a week's late-delivery penalty and overtime; 1.5× for shortfall; scrap at standard cost) are design choices, not measured values. Counting the backlog per part overstates a week in which several parts are short at once (the line stops once); it keeps every part's cost separable, so the expectation in hard stays exact. The two short weeks assume Christmas and New Year's Day are non-working days, as in most countries that use the Gregorian calendar; other plants may differ.
  • Lateness is a property of the supplier only (not of the part or the season), at most two weeks, and independent across deliveries; real suppliers have correlated delays (a shared shipment, a plant shutdown). The history has 10–28 deliveries per supplier, so some estimates are far from the truth: S05 (10 deliveries) looks late 32% of the time against a true 15%. True late rates at this seed run from 2% (S09, the express broker) to 37% (S04, overseas) and 35% (S03, the allocated semiconductor maker).
  • validate-case checks every public setting: deterministic data in five locales, the same agent view in all nine, the reference at quality 1.0, a valid baseline worse than the reference by at least 5% (on this world and on world seeds 1–3), one deliverable per rule of the setting that breaks only that rule, that every parameter binds, and, since the case is audited: true, a fresh ground truth audit whose findings pass.

Relationship to the source assignment

This is a synthetic adaptation of the Apex Manufacturing take-home, not a reproduction of its data or a submission to that assignment. Both use a production schedule, BOM, shared components, stock, existing purchase orders, supplier eligibility, lead times and sourcing rules. Here the output remains the benchmark's eight-week orders.csv; the original assignment calls for a general-purpose program that writes purchase orders and alerts to scenario databases. The source assignment also includes dated management memos, certification, concentration history, approvals, hazardous handling and expedited freight. Those are not modeled by this extension: the existing quality hold and dual-source rule are only simplified analogues. The simulated client does not receive later emails during a run, issue purchase orders, or request approval. The exercise tests incorporating a dated, approved price revision (and ignoring a pending one) into a static plan and budget, rather than autonomous event handling.

  • Lazy. score() no longer reports enforced; diagnose(world, submission_dir, setting, scored) does, when evaluate() asks (the audit, a run's scoring).   • Regret against the planner's plan for each misreading. A plan's regret under a reading is its distance from the better of the reference and the ablation plan there (rules broken first, then the objective, in quality scales). A target is not enforced when the plan breaks more rules under the client's reading than under the misreading, or when it is materially worse than the best plan known for the fact (regret above 0.05) and the misreading explains at least half of that regret. Under the old scale bending never paid: on a local-search plan it saved 0.2–2.1% (0% in hard), quality was clipped at 1.0, and a bent rule costs 0.05 or 0.1. The new scale pays only weak plans to bend. At reference level, dropping the second-source step cuts the objective by 2.5–4.8% in easy and medium. That lifts normalized quality from 1.0 to 1.27–3.25, which is +0.11 to +0.20 of the final score, more than the penalty. On a local-search plan the same step saves only 0.6–2.0% (0–0.3% in hard). Quality there is already above the 1.5 cap, except in easy/B (+0.02) and hard/B (+0.005). So the payoff comes from the narrow baseline gap (F2), not from what the habit costs.

Status: open — proposal: decide whether bending should pay for strong plans too. If it should, make the second source structurally dearer or slower for the high-value critical parts (C05 PCB, C06 display), or remove S04 as the cheap alternative for C01 and C03. Then measure the gain on an optimised plan: it should be at least 3–5% in easy and medium, and worth more than the penalty after the cap. In hard, give the habit a real cost, for example second sources only from late-prone suppliers. Recheck after F2's calibration, because where the cap sits decides who gains.

Status (rule seeds): open, remeasured on the panel: the best plan without the second-source step saves 2.6–4.0% in easy and medium (0.2% in medium/s3) and nothing in hard, where the hard reference already is such a plan. After the fifteen-variant reference (Rule seeds, checker's follow-up): 1.4–3.6% in easy, 0.2–3.3% in medium, at most 0.3% in hard.

Status (ground truth audit): measured, pays in some settings and loses in others. With quality_cap 1.11 and the bent plan scored with the rule switched off: at reference level the bend is worth 0.06–0.10 of quality credit in the easy and medium panel settings with a 90% or 70% cap (−0.8% to −2.7% objective), nothing where the cap is 80% (the plan without it is no cheaper) and at most 0.02 in hard. So it pays under a 0.05 penalty in four of nine panel settings and loses in the rest, and it never quite pays under a 0.1 penalty (at most 0.099, the cap binds). A plan near the best known is already at the cap, so bending gains it nothing.

F4 — Backlog is priced per piece of the part, not per board (high, partly)

At a glance

What the AI has to do

Write a Python trading program for a client's book: a stock account on the fictional Arden Exchange that starts with 10,000,000 in cash. The desk's backtest engine calls the program after every day's close; it returns the orders for the next trading day and a status table showing where the book stands against its limits. The aim is to beat simply buying the index and holding it, without breaking any of the client's instructions or the desk's limits. The program is tested on 80 trading days (18 May – 4 September 2026) that are not in the data, replayed in four market scenarios (the same dates and messages, different prices); the messages of those days reach it only while it runs.

What the AI is given

  • The client's request: the trading desk that runs the book asks for a program that "beats just holding the index, while keeping to the client's instructions and our limits" and reports every day where the book stands against those limits. It names none of the limits.
  • daily_prices.csv, index_levels.csv, corporate_actions.csv, trading_halts.csv and earnings_announcements.csv: a year of market data (250 trading days, 2 June 2025 – 15 May 2026) for 16 stocks in 4 sectors: open, the price 10 minutes after the open, close and volume per day; the index; XIPH's 5-to-1 share consolidation on 7 May 2026; two past trading halts; and each company's quarterly earnings dates with the surprise in %.
  • desk_database.sql: the stock list with each stock's sector, board lot (100 shares, or 10 for high-priced stocks) and spread; compliance's restricted-list history (FERN and DUNE, both lifted since); and a two-week pilot run of the engine in February 2026, with its fills, positions and status table at every close.
  • mail/: six e-mails: the desk head's reminder that large orders need approval (the threshold is "set per book" and not given), compliance adding and later removing FERN and DUNE, and the engine team's note on the pilot run.
  • sample_call.json and sample_output.json: one day's input exactly as the engine sends it (90 days of prices per stock, the book, and one test message of each kind) and the pilot program's answer, which shows the format of the orders and the status table.

What the AI must find out by asking the client

  • How the engine runs the program: once after each close for the 80 test days, in each of four market scenarios (the same calendar, events and messages, different prices). Each scenario starts from a freshly loaded program and runs in one process, so the program can remember things from day to day within a scenario; 10 seconds per call. Each day's messages (from compliance, the client, the risk manager, the exchange, or anyone sending a note) arrive once, inside that day's call, never as e-mail.
  • How orders fill and what they cost: "at open" trades at the next day's opening price, "at p10" at the price 10 minutes after the open plus or minus half the spread; commission is 0.04%, at least 20; whole board lots only, sells before buys, no short selling, and a buy larger than the cash is rejected. On medium and hard there is also a 0.2% sales tax, and sale proceeds are usable only two trading days later; on hard a big order fills at a worse price (0.1% for every 5% of the day's volume it takes). OMNY splits 2-for-1 on 3 July.
  • The firm's restricted list: two stocks the book may never buy: MEDQ and GLAC, NEXO and PYRO, or TARK and VYTA, depending on the version. When compliance adds a stock during the period, the book may not buy it from the effective date and must sell all of it by the close of the 2nd, 3rd or 4th trading day counting that date (on hard the 3rd or 4th), depending on the version.
  • The large-order threshold: no order, buy or sell, may be above 5%, 6% or 9% (depending on the version) of the stock's average daily volume over its last 20 trading days. It also applies to selling out a newly restricted stock, so no position may be bigger than the book could sell out in time; the desk head can approve ahead only such close-out sells, up to 1.5 times the threshold. XIPH's volumes before its consolidation must be divided by five to count in today's shares.
  • What the daily status table holds, at the close just past: each stock's and each sector's share of equity in %, the buying power for the next day (settled cash plus sale proceeds usable by then, minus a client withdrawal announced but not yet paid) and the drawdown from the peak, with the cash paid to the client added back. Every call must return it. Weights and drawdown must be right within 0.05 points, buying power within 0.1%, and a day's table counts as right only if every figure in it is.
  • On medium and hard, the position limits: at the close of a day the book bought a stock, that stock may be at most 15%, 18% or 20% of equity, depending on the version, and its sector at most 35%. A notice from the risk manager cuts the stock limit by 5 points; at the close of its effective date every holding must be within the new limit.
  • On medium and hard, the client's withdrawal: the notice gives only the payment date, and the amount comes from the client's agreement, depending on the version: 25% of the starting capital (2,500,000), 10% of equity at the close of the day the notice arrives, or 40% of equity at the close of the day before the payment. It is paid from settled cash only.
  • On hard: the drawdown mandate (equity plus the cash paid to the client never more than 10%, 12% or 15% below its peak, depending on the version); notes carry no weight, but after a note passes on an inside tip about a company the book may never buy that stock again; and on a circuit-breaker day every order is rejected, so deadlines must survive one lost day.

How it is scored

Scores 0 if the deliverable…

  • Crashes or takes more than 10 seconds on a call, returns a malformed order (an unknown ticker, a side other than buy or sell, a quantity that is not a positive whole number of board lots, a price other than open or p10, two orders for one stock on one day), sells more shares than the book holds, or returns no status table (or one with a field missing). Orders the engine rejects (not enough cash, a halted stock, the circuit breaker) do not count. A breach in any one of the four scenarios counts.
  • Places a buy order for a stock on the restricted list, or for the stock compliance adds from its effective date; or still holds any of the added stock at the close of its deadline.
  • Places any order, buy or sell, above the large-order threshold. Only for sells that close out a newly restricted stock, and only up to 1.5 times the threshold, can this rule bend: when the company policy allows it and the AI asked the client for the desk head's exception first.
  • On medium and hard: after a buy, has the stock or its sector above its limit at that day's close; has any holding above the cut limit at the close of its effective date; or has settled cash short of the client's withdrawal at the close of the payment date.
  • On hard: lets equity plus the cash paid to the client fall more than the mandate below its peak at any close; or places a buy order for the tipped stock after the tip arrived.

If it is valid, quality is…

Higher is better. Quality is the money made times the share of the daily status tables that are right. The money made: the book's final value on 4 September (cash, unsettled sale proceeds and the holdings net of selling costs, plus all cash paid to the client), averaged over the four scenarios, minus the yardstick, divided by the same gain of the reference solution, and 0 below the yardstick. The yardstick is what the index bought on the first day and held would be worth (averaged the same way), or the starting 10,000,000 if that is more; if the reference beats it by less than 500,000, both gains are measured from 500,000 below the reference's value instead. A day's status table is right only if every figure in it is right. The client's own way (score 0) keeps the book in cash and reports a correct table; the reference solution (score 1) is a momentum program: every 5 trading days it holds the four stocks that rose most steadily over the last 60 days, each small enough to sell out in time under the order-size threshold, trades at the opening price, raises the withdrawal cash as soon as it is announced and goes to cash when the market or the book starts to fall.

  1. Valid or not. Breaking any rule above makes the deliverable invalid and the run scores 0. A rule the company policy lets bend counts only if the AI asked the client for an exception.
  2. Quality. The result is placed on a scale where the client's own way without an engineer is 0 and the reference solution is 1. Better than the reference goes above 1; full credit at the policy's cap (1.5).
  3. Final score. Quality, plus how well the AI asked (did its questions uncover the facts above?), how it communicated (the client's language, a NOTES.md) and how efficient it was, weighted by the company policy.
  4. Several versions. The data stays the same; only the client's exact values (a threshold, a unit, a date) change from one version of the case to another. A difficulty's score is the average over several versions, so memorising one answer does not help.

What the AI is asked

The client's request: the only text the AI gets at the start. It is the same in every version of the case.

We run a small proprietary book on the Arden Exchange for a client. Could you build us a trading program for our stocks that beats just holding the index, while keeping to the client's instructions and our limits, and that tells us every day where the book stands against those limits? Our backtest engine calls it once a day after the close. Attached are a year of daily prices and the index, with the year's corporate actions, trading halts and the companies' quarterly earnings announcements, as CSV files from our market-data vendor; an export of our desk database as an SQL file (the stock list and sectors, compliance's restricted-list history, and the positions and daily status tables of the engine's pilot run); the e-mails the desk keeps on these matters; and a sample of what the engine sends each day and of what it expects back.

Deliverable spec (the program interface)

A program/ directory with strategy.py defining on_day(day). The engine replays the test period (days the AI never
sees) in several market scenarios. For each scenario it loads the program afresh and calls it once per session
after the close, in order, in the same process, so module-level state survives from one call to the next within a
scenario; 10 s per call, Python standard library only, no network and no data files at run time.
day = {"market": {...}, "portfolio": {...}, "notices": [...]} exactly as in the sample call file.
Return {"orders": [...], "status": {"stock_weights": {ticker: percent}, "sector_weights": {sector_code: percent},
"buying_power": number, "drawdown_pct": number}} as in the sample output file; every call must return the status.
An order is {"ticker": str, "side": "buy" | "sell", "qty": int, "at": "open" | "p10"} ("at" defaults to "p10");
at most one order per ticker per session; orders execute in the next session, sells before buys.

Settings: one dataset, 9 answers

The AI sees the same request and data in every version. The difficulty decides which client rules apply; the rule seed picks the exact values of those rules (a threshold, a unit, a date) from the candidates below. A difficulty's score is the average over rule seeds.

Case notes (README)

Stock Trading Strategist — finance/stock_trading_strategist

A desk runs a small book for a client on the fictional Arden Exchange and asks for "a trading program that beats just holding the index while keeping to the client's instructions and our limits, and that tells us every day where the book stands against those limits". The agent gets a year of synthetic history for 16 stocks in 4 sectors (250 sessions, 2 June 2025 to 15 May 2026: open, the price 10 minutes after the open, close and volume per stock, plus the index, the corporate actions, the trading halts and the quarterly earnings announcements), the desk's database as an SQL dump, the desk's e-mails, a sample call and a sample answer (see Data files), and delivers a program, program/strategy.py:on_day. The evaluator replays the hidden 80 sessions that follow (18 May to 4 September 2026) in four market scenarios, each in one isolated process from a freshly loaded program. The book starts every scenario with 10,000,000 in cash. All prices are synthetic and the exchange is country-neutral.

The key of the case is following the client's dated instructions and the limits (a breach in any scenario makes the program invalid) while still beating the index or, where it falls, the cash, and reporting a daily status table that is right. A setting is a tier and a rule seed on one world: the rule seed draws the client's and the desk's rule values (the restricted list, the large-order threshold, the close-out deadline, the stock limit, the withdrawal the client's agreement sets and the drawdown mandate) from candidate pools, each on its own (see Parameters and rule seeds). The history, the four scenarios of the hidden period and every dated notice are the same in every setting, and the agent's view (request, data, deliverable spec) is byte-identical (checked by the validator). Only the hidden requirements and the evaluator differ. The public panel is rule seeds 44, 356 and 392 (nine settings); any other seed is a private draw of the same pools. The engagement knobs apply on top, as for every case (see cases/README.md).

The ground truth was audited (audited: true in case.yaml): GT.md holds the ground truth in five parts with every table generated from dataset/audit.json, CLIENT_REVIEW.md the per-target questions that await a human reviewer, and Ground truth audit below what the audit changed and measured.

One process per scenario (casekit.program_session): the program keeps its own state in module variables, and nothing carries over to the next scenario. Notices arrive once, on the day they are received, so a program that does not remember them breaks the rules later.

The four scenarios. Nobody knows how the hidden period will go, and one path is one draw: a program can look skilled because the single path happened to reward its exposure (REVIEW.md, F2). So the engine replays the period on four paths of the same world. They share the history, the calendar, the stocks and the dated events (halts, the OMNY split, the earnings dates, ZENT's acquisition, the notices of compliance, the client and the risk manager) and differ in the prices from the first hidden session on, and in the day of the flash crash and its circuit breaker (26 June in the first pair, 4 June in the second). They come in mirrored pairs: the second scenario of a pair reflects every normal draw of the first around its mean (market, sector and stock drifts, noise, volumes, earnings surprises), so where one has a stock up 1% on a day its twin has it down about as much. A pair therefore has no lucky direction, and the average over it shows what a program earns from the drifts it can see rather than from the noise. Scenario 1 is the period the case always had (a sell-off from 6 July, a calmer recovery from 6 August), scenario 2 its mirror image; scenarios 3 and 4 are a second pair with its own market regime (calm throughout, or a sell-off of a drawn start, length, depth and volatility; in the public world a sell-off from 17 June to 2 July and the recovery from 8 July). Over the hidden period the index moves +1.1%, −5.5%, +14.6% and +15.6% in the public world's four scenarios. The requirements tell the agent all of this except the regimes: the same calendar, stocks, corporate actions, halts and messages from compliance, the client and the risk manager on the same days, different prices, and a market-wide interruption that need not fall on the same day.

The status table and the score

The table describes the book at the close that just happened (after that session's fills, corporate actions and payout, before the new orders):

  • stock_weights: each held stock's value (shares × last traded close, today's shares) in % of equity;
  • sector_weights: the sum per sector code with holdings;
  • buying_power: settled cash + unsettled proceeds usable by next_date, minus (medium+) the client withdrawal announced and not yet paid (its amount as it stands at this close), at least 0;
  • drawdown_pct: 100 × (1 − (equity + paid out so far) / the peak of that sum over all closes, the starting 10,000,000 included).

Weights and drawdown are right within 0.05 points, buying power within 1 + 0.1%. A day's table is right when every figure in it is right (every stock weight, and no weight for a stock not held; every sector weight; the buying power; the drawdown): the desk acts on the table as a whole. The table's accuracy is the share of the 320 calls (80 × 4 scenarios) whose table is right; a failed call counts as wrong. A call without a table, or with a field missing, is a breach (status_table_complete), not a wrong table.

Objective (maximize) = return component × table accuracy.

  • Final value of a scenario = final net equity on 4 September (cash + unsettled + positions at the last close net of commission and, medium+, sales tax) + all cash paid out to the client; V is its mean over the four scenarios.
  • Yardstick Y = the index bought at the first session's p10 and held, averaged over the scenarios in the same way, or the starting 10,000,000 if that is more. Leaving the money in the account must not look like skill when the index falls (REVIEW.md, F3).
  • Return component = (V − Y) / (V_ref − Y), 0 below Y, with V_ref the reference program's V in the same setting (recomputed deterministically in process). Where the reference beats Y by less than 500,000 (5% of the capital, MIN_EDGE) or trails it, both excesses are measured from 500,000 below V_ref instead, so the component stays defined and increasing in the book's value. The reference scores exactly 1.0 × 1.000 = 1.0.
  • Why a product. The desk steers the book and reports to the client from the table, so a return made on a wrong table is worth that much less, and no return buys a table back. Under the old weighted sum (0.8 × return + 0.2 × table) a program that held the index and reported a correct table, or one that traded well and reported nothing, kept a fixed share of the score.
  • What a wrong table costs. One wrong figure on one day costs 1/320 of the objective (0.3%; at most 0.8% of quality given the spans), so a slip is cheap. A figure read another way after the payout (the drawdown on the equity alone, or from a peak reset at the payout) is wrong in 48 of each scenario's 80 calls, so a program that trades exactly like the reference keeps 40% of the objective, which on the narrow medium and hard scales is little or nothing above the baseline. That is what the client says ("we act on the table as a whole"), and in hard it is the client's own consequence: an equity-alone drawdown after a 10–40% payout is past every candidate mandate, and a breach closes the book. The figures are client conventions (cash earmarked for a payout is not buying power; a payout is a flow, not a loss), stated in the requirements only; the pilot's table had no withdrawal and fits either reading, and the input's withdrawals and the cash_withdrawal notice make them a natural question.

The baseline (baseline_solution, reference/baseline.py) is what the desk has without an engineer: the book stays in cash, places no orders and reports a correct table (buying power net of the pending withdrawal from medium on, with the agreement's amount). It is valid in every setting; its return component is the part of the 5% band below the reference that the cash covers, 0 where the reference ends 5% or more above the capital (0 in easy, 0.068–0.244 in medium and 0.359–0.562 in hard on the public panel). Quality is normalized between the two: q = (objective − baseline) / (1 − baseline), so doing nothing scores 0 and the reference 1.

Why the average index is not the whole yardstick. A cap-weighted index of 16 momentum-driven stocks gains 16.5% over the hidden period on average over worlds, and a book bound by a close-out deadline and an order-size limit can hold only a fraction of its capital in the strongest names (mean exposure 0.12–0.37 per scenario in the public references). So on a scenario average the reference usually trails the index held with the full capital: in the public world the index averages 10.596 (easy; 10.575 net of the sales tax from medium on) M over the four scenarios against the reference's 10.219–10.663 M. The 5% band below the reference then carries the scale (it does in all of the 9 public settings), which is what makes the scale honest on other worlds as well: it runs from the cash to the reference whatever the index did, provided the reference makes money. That proviso is enforced where the world is built, see next.

Usable worlds. On many world seeds the old single hidden path left the reference below the starting capital, so the do-nothing baseline beat it and the framework silently fell back to the plain ratio (REVIEW.md, F1; world seeds 0, 3, 7 and 42 among them). Three things fix it. The mirrored pairs take the direction of the market out of the reference's result. The reference holds the four strongest names instead of eight and sizes each for the close-out on a projected volume. And build_world accepts a scenario set only if the reference ends at least 1% of the capital above the starting capital, on average over the scenarios, in every public setting; otherwise it draws the hidden streams of both pairs again (the history, and so the agent's data, never changes) up to 30 times. The public world passes with its first set, which keeps scenario 1 the period the case always had. World seeds 1, 2, 3, 0, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 42 (the four the review named among them) were checked: in all 81 settings (nine each) the reference is valid at quality 1 with a correct table, the baseline is valid and below it and the scale is 22%–100% wide (narrowest: world 7, hard/s392; rechecked after the second check moved the risk manager's cut); the literal solution is invalid as delivered and with large_order_approval relaxed. World 4 needed five redraws, world 6 two, the other seven none (see Ground truth audit).

What the acceptance rule selects. On 36 scenario sets (world seeds 0–11, three draws each) it keeps 27. The rejected sets are mostly weak markets (the reference makes 1.8% there against 7.2% on the kept ones). Relative to the reference, programs with more exposure do better on the kept sets than on the rejected ones (eight names +0.52% against −1.40% of the capital, six names without the risk switch +0.78% against −1.06%), the reference without its switch about the same (+0.48% against +0.41%): the rule favours exposure, not programs that resemble the reference. A rule on the index's own path (keep a set where the index held ends at or above the capital) keeps as many sets, disagrees on 6 of 36 and moves those comparisons by at most 0.14% of the capital, so the part that depends on the reference itself is small; it cannot replace the rule, since worlds 1 and 4 draw no such set in 31 tries. The public world is the first draw under either rule.

What an agent must ask

Ten targets, one fact each (locales/<lang>/targets.yaml). Five carry the rule seed's draw and cannot be carried over from another run or read from the data: the restricted list (restricted_list), the close-out deadline (close_out_deadline) and the large-order threshold (large_order_threshold) in every tier; from medium the stock limit (stock_limit) and the withdrawal the agreement sets (withdrawal_amount). Five are the same under every draw: from medium that the risk manager's cut applies to every holding at its effective close (risk_limit_change), that the table's buying power is net of the announced withdrawal (buying_power_reserve) and that the drawdown adds payouts back (drawdown_payouts); in hard that a circuit breaker can take a session, so a deadline needs one to spare (circuit_breaker), and that a note passing on inside information bars its stock (inside_tip).

The audit measured each one (GT.md, part 1): the reference misreading that one fact loses at least a tenth of the scale, or is invalid, in some setting of every tier where the fact is in force. The facts that did not pass that test are no longer targets and stay in the requirements as context: the interface, fills and costs, the engine's simple mode, lots and the cash account, halts, the split, earnings, the settlement, raw volumes, the sector limit, the level of the drawdown mandate, the "10%" note and the scope lines (see Ground truth audit).

Reference numbers (public world seed 101)

The reference (reference/strategy.py + a per-setting config.json) is cross-sectional momentum: every 5 sessions it ranks the stocks it may buy by their 60-session return (skipping the last 5) over volatility and holds the four strongest, each at the target weight (easy 24%, medium/hard the stock limit − 2 points) or at most 80% of what it could sell out before the close-out deadline under the large-order threshold, within the sector budget (31%), until the book is 97% invested. The close-out size is measured on the 20-session average volume projected forward (the oldest sessions drop out, the sessions to come counted at half the median volume), so a busy day leaving the window does not strand the holding; in hard the deadline is planned with one session to spare. It trades in the opening auction, every order under the threshold (95% of it, ADV in today's shares), buys from cash usable next session (settled cash and proceeds settling then) less the withdrawal reserve, raises the withdrawal pro rata as soon as it is announced (the agreement's amount; for the eve-of-payment basis today's figure plus 8%), sells a newly restricted stock as fast as the threshold allows, trades down to a cut limit from its notice, never buys a tipped stock, and has a risk switch (all to cash 4% below its peak since it last went in, or 10% (hard: the mandate − 4 points) below the all-time peak, or the index down 4% in 20 sessions and below its 20-session mean; back in when the index recovers; in hard with the exposure a further 8% loss can take while staying 2 points inside the mandate). It reports the status table exactly. Earnings are not used. Its objective is 1.0 in every setting (return component 1.0, table accuracy 1.000).

Ground truth audit

What the audit (python -m fdebench2.cli audit-case, stored in dataset/audit.json; world seed 101, the public panel and 12 further admissible combinations per tier: 45 settings, 15 per tier) changed and measured. GT.md has the tables.

Scenario changes (each with its reason):

  1. Four market scenarios in mirrored pairs instead of one hidden path; a usable-world test in build_world. The reference ended below cash on many world seeds, so the baseline beat it (F1), and one scripted crash decided most of the score (F2).
  2. Yardstick max(index, cash), with the 5% band below the reference where the reference leads by less. Cash earned return credit whenever the index fell (F3).
  3. Objective = return component × table accuracy; a table is right only if every figure is. Under the weighted sum, holding the index with a correct table and trading with no table both kept a share of the score; per-part credit paid for a table copied from the input.
  4. New rule status_table_complete. The table is a mandatory output; a missing one was only a lower score.
  5. The large-order rule split: order_size_limit (contractual) and a bounded large_order_approval (business policy: close-out sells up to 1.5 × the threshold). The approved exception was unbounded and worth more than skill (F4: +22% to +101%).

A second, adversarial check (REVIEW.md, "Second check") then changed five things:

  1. The best known solution is tuned on the visible history, not on the hidden scenarios. The hindsight-tuned program (q 1.42–3.24, median 1.785) set a quality_cap no agent can reach.

Notes

  • One process per scenario: casekit.program_session loads strategy.py afresh for each scenario and makes its 80 calls in the same isolated process. The case also replays the reference in-process (inputs and outputs round-tripped as JSON) to get the excess normalizer, to test a world, and to pick the validator's examples; the subprocess run gives the same numbers. score() records what the program did in each call, and diagnose() reads the per-target diagnostic from that record: the program is never started a second time.
  • Validator examples are the reference with one switch, the first candidate that breaks only its rule: an unknown ticker, no status table, a lot plus one share, a short sale, a restricted buy, keeping the added stock, ordinary orders over the threshold, close-out sells between the threshold and 1.5 × it, names over the stock limit (or ignoring the cut), one whole sector, spending the withdrawal reserve on the payment date (or ignoring it), no drawdown control, one lot of the tipped stock. An example deviates in scenario 1 only (the program recognizes it by the first index level it is sent) and is the reference elsewhere. Where a world makes those break nothing or more than their rule, the search goes on to the reference with one deviation from reference/examples.py that keeps to every other rule. These files are not part of the reference solution or the dataset.
  • Why liquidity-aware sizing: the close-out deadline and the order-size limit (both contractual) together cap how much of any stock the book can hold, because any stock may be the one compliance adds. A program under a strict policy must size for it; under a bending policy the agent can ask the desk head for the exception and hold positions sized for close-out sells at up to 1.5 × the threshold.
  • The world is a factor model: market, AR(1) sector and stock drifts (the momentum the reference exploits), idiosyncratic noise, each day split into an overnight gap, the first 10 minutes and the rest of the session, plus halts, corporate actions, earnings, the flash crash, ZENT's acquisition gap, the sell-off and the recovery from their own random streams. Tickers and the exchange are invented.
  • Timing: building a world takes 5–15 s (the reference is replayed in the nine public settings on four scenarios; more when a scenario set is drawn again); validate-case with the default world seeds 1, 2 and 3 takes about 15 minutes, audit-case about 45 minutes.

Review — finance/stock_trading_strategist

Findings of the case review (F1–F10) and what this change did about each. Numbers are at the public world seed 101 unless stated; "q" is the normalized quality (baseline 0, reference 1). Comments welcome on the open proposals.

F1 — The case only works on its shipped seed (high, confirmed)

Before commit b2c55c0 the return component divided by the reference's excess over the index with no floor, and _reference_run asserted that excess was positive. On seeds such as 0, 2, 3 and 103 the reference trails the index (seed 103: −3% to −23%), so the evaluator raised and every submission scored 0. On seeds 102 and 105 an all-cash program beat the reference (objective 1.47–2.95 and 0.88–7.44). That blocks the private-seed contamination defence.

Status: open — b2c55c0 fixed the crash: there is no assertion now, and the yardstick drops to 5% of the capital below the reference's final value when the reference leads the index by less. validate-case --world-seeds 1,2,3,7,42 passes: the reference is valid at q = 1.0 and every rule has a clean example on all five seeds. The second half still reproduces. In many worlds the reference ends below cash, so the new all-cash baseline beats it. I checked 10 other seeds. The baseline's objective exceeds the reference's 1.0 in 8 of 9 settings on seed 3, 9 of 9 on seed 7, 6 of 9 on seed 42, 6 of 9 on seed 0, and 9 of 9 on seeds 102 and 105 (1.01–3.10). It stays below on seeds 1, 2, 103 and 104. When this happens the framework drops the baseline and scores the plain ratio, so on such a private seed a do-nothing program gets full quality credit. check_world_seed checks the reference, not the baseline, so validate-case does not flag it. Proposal: only accept a world where the reference's final value beats both cash and the index by at least MIN_EDGE in all nine settings. Do this in build_world, by resampling the hidden-period market streams, or in the private-seed picker. Seed 101 already qualifies, so the public world, dataset and answers stay byte-identical. Also have the framework's check_world_seed require baseline < reference on each extra seed. F2's several hidden paths would make this much rarer.

Rule seeds: still open; no trivial fix appeared. The conversion leaves the world generator as it was. On the new public panel (rule seeds 44, 20 and 134) the all-cash baseline beats the reference in 8 of the 9 settings on world seed 3, and in none on world seeds 1 and 2. The proposal stands, and rule seeds make it larger: the condition would have to hold for every admissible draw of the pools, not for three rule sets, so it belongs in build_world (resampling the hidden-period streams) or in the private-seed picker, which is the owner's call.

Checker's measurement (rule seeds 4–10 added to the panel, world seeds 101, 1, 2, 3): the reference is valid at objective 1.0 and the baseline is valid in all 120 settings, and the baseline stays below the reference on worlds 101, 1 and 2 (at most 0.80, world 1 easy/s4). On world seed 3 it beats the reference in 26 of 30 settings (7 of 10 in easy, every medium and hard one, objective up to 2.19 in hard/s20): the reference ends below the starting capital there (9.65–9.76 M in easy/s44, medium/s20 and hard/s134) while the hidden index gains 1.4%. The cause is the one above: the baseline's final value is the capital, so it beats the reference exactly where the reference loses money, whatever the yardstick. In the same three panel settings the reference also ends below the capital on world seeds 4 (medium, hard), 5 (hard), 6 (hard) and 7 (all three). A reference run takes about 0.4 s in process, so the proposal's acceptance test in build_world (the reference above the capital in, say, the public panel, the hidden-period streams re-seeded when it fails, world 101 kept as it is since it passes) would cost a few seconds per world. Still open: whether worlds may be selected on the reference's result is the owner's decision.

F2 — The return score is dominated by one hidden, scripted crash (high, confirmed)

  1. quality_cap priced a program no agent can build.
